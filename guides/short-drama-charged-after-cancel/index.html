Step one: find out who billed you

Most failed cancellations are not failed. They are cancellations of the wrong route.

If you subscribed through Google Play and cancelled inside the app, you cancelled the right thing. If you subscribed through the app’s own website and cancelled inside the app, you did not — and no amount of repeating that same action will help.

So the first move is to read the transaction, not the app:

Why the descriptor will not match the app

App publishers in this category frequently bill through a separate legal entity — a regional company, a payment subsidiary, or a processor’s own descriptor. A descriptor that has nothing to do with the app name is common and is not by itself evidence of fraud. What tells you more is whether the charge recurs and whether it matches a subscription you meant to cancel.

The four billing routes and four different fixes

1. Apple App Store. Cancelling in iOS Settings works, and in-app cancelling usually works too because they reach the same subscription. The store is the easiest of the four to deal with.

2. Google Play. Same arrangement. Cancel in the app or through the Play Store. One subtlety worth knowing: Google Play pre-authorises a monthly subscription up to two days before the renewal date, so a pending hold can appear after you cancel — that is the pre-authorisation, not a second live subscription.

3. The app’s own website. This is the one people forget exists. Our ReelShort cancellation guide covers the three routes in detail, and the web checkout is the reason the problem is as common as it is.

4. PayPal. Cancelled entirely inside PayPal. Details below.

ReelShort runs its own web checkout

This is not unusual for the app; it is what makes the failure mode so common.

We verified on 10 October 2026 that ReelShort runs a web checkout taking cards, Google Pay, PayPal and bank transfer directly, outside the store. A weekly VIP came to $11.87 including tax there.

A subscription bought through that page is invisible to Google Play. So:

  • cancelling inside the ReelShort app cancels the Play subscription, not this one
  • cancelling at Google Play does not touch it either
  • the only route that works is ReelShort’s own site or support

To be clear about the risk: it is a normal payment processor, not a stranger, and the checkout is run by the publisher. The problem is not that it is shady — it is that store subscriptions are cancellable by you, while web subscriptions are cancellable only by the company. That asymmetry is the whole issue.

If you subscribed on the web, do not bother with the app

Find the confirmation email or the order in your card statement, and cancel through ReelShort’s own site or support. Repeating the in-app cancellation will not reach it, and each extra attempt costs you time you may need for the refund.

PayPal is charged outside the app entirely

PayPal needs its own treatment, and the failure mode here is specific: users report cancelling on the app’s own website and still being charged by PayPal.

That is not a bug on either side. The website cancellation and the PayPal authorisation are two separate records. If you signed up through PayPal, the live authorisation sits in your PayPal account, and the developer has no button that can switch it off.

The route that works:

  1. Log into PayPal
  2. Settings, then Payments
  3. Manage automatic payments or Manage saved merchants
  4. Find the merchant and cancel the authorisation

Do that first if PayPal is the route. It is the one action that reliably stops the charge, and it does not depend on the app company responding to an email.

What to screenshot, and when

Take the screenshot immediately after cancelling, before you close the app. It is the fastest evidence you can hold, and every refund or dispute process downstream will ask for it.

Four things to capture in one go:

  • the confirmation that the cancellation went through — the message or the changed subscription state
  • the date the access runs until, if shown
  • the route you cancelled through (the store screen, or the confirmation email)
  • the last charge before the cancellation, from your statement

One detail worth knowing, because it tells you whether a cancellation actually took: the subscription entry does not disappear when you cancel — the date changes. An active subscription shows a next billing date; a cancelled one shows an access-until date. If you still see a future billing date after cancelling, the cancellation did not go through.

Keep the screenshot even when nothing is wrong

If a charge lands after a confirmed cancellation, this screenshot is the difference between an instant resolution and a multi-week dispute. It costs one second to take while you are already on the screen.

If the charge still lands

If you cancelled, have the confirmation, and the charge still appears: this is no longer a settings problem and no amount of further cancelling will fix it.

Go to whoever actually took the money:

  • Store-billed → request a refund through the store
  • Web-checkout or PayPal-billed → the app company or the processor, with your cancellation screenshot attached
  • Unrecognised and recurring → ask your bank

A chargeback through your bank is the last resort. It works, it takes weeks, and it can cost you the ability to use the payment method afterwards — so exhaust the direct route first, and go in with your cancellation screenshot.

Bottom line

The reason people keep getting charged after cancelling is almost never a bug. It is that they cancelled one route while the money went through another.

Identify the descriptor first. apple.com/bill means Apple. GOOGLE* means the Play Store. A company name you do not recognise means the publisher or a processor, and the app and the store cannot cancel it for you. PayPal means PayPal, and only PayPal.

Then screenshot the confirmation while you are still on the screen — the entry staying in the list with an access-until date instead of a billing date is how you know it worked.

Frequently asked questions

Why am I still being charged after cancelling a short drama app subscription?

Usually because the cancellation was done through a different route than the one that took the payment. An Apple charge reads apple.com/bill, a Google Play charge starts with GOOGLE followed by an asterisk, and anything else was billed by the app company or a payment processor directly — possibly under a company name that has nothing to do with the app. Cancelling inside the app does not cancel a web-checkout subscription, and cancelling on the developer website does not cancel a store subscription.

How do I find out who charged me?

Look at the descriptor on your bank or card statement, not the app name. Apple uses apple.com/bill. Google Play uses a description starting with GOOGLE followed by an asterisk and then the publisher name. Anything else is the app company or a payment processor, and the descriptor may be a company name unrelated to the app you subscribed to.

I cancelled on the app website but PayPal still charged me. Why?

Because the website cancellation and the PayPal authorisation are two separate records. If you signed up through PayPal, the live authorisation sits in your PayPal account under automatic payments, not on the developer site. Cancelling there does not touch it. You need to cancel the automatic payment in PayPal itself: Settings, then Payments, then Manage automatic payments or Manage saved merchants, find the merchant, and cancel it.

Will a refund work if I was double charged?

It depends entirely on who took the money, and the route differs. Store-billed purchases can be refunded through the store. A web-checkout or PayPal purchase has to go through the app company or the payment processor, and neither is obliged to act quickly. A chargeback through your bank is the last resort and takes weeks. Screenshot the cancellation confirmation as soon as you have it — that is the evidence the refund or dispute process will ask for.

The statement shows a company name I have never heard of. Is that fraud?

Not necessarily. App publishers of this category frequently operate through separate shell or regional companies, so the descriptor is often a legal entity with no connection to the brand name in your app drawer. That is not by itself evidence of fraud. Check whether the charge is recurring and whether it matches a subscription you meant to cancel before assuming the worst — and if you cannot match it at all, ask your bank rather than the app.
